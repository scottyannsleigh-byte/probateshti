1) ADVERSARY PROCEEDING UNDER RULE 5.025. A dispute over whether real property belongs to the estate versus a deed grantee is not one of the automatically-adversary proceedings listed in Fla. Prob. R. 5.025(a) (that list covers removal/surcharge of a PR, will construction/reformation, determination of beneficiaries, revocation of probate, elective share, etc. — see https://floridarules.net/probate/rule-5-025-adversary-proceedings/ and https://www.syfert.com/florida/probate-rules/rule-5.025/). However, under Rule 5.025(b) "other proceedings may be declared adversary" by serving a separate declaration of adversary status with the petition, and under 5.025(c) the court may declare any proceeding adversary at any time. Mechanics: she would file a verified petition (Rule 5.020 pleading standards) plus a declaration that the proceeding is adversary, and serve FORMAL NOTICE (Rule 5.040) on the PR and interested persons — formal notice is served like process. Under Rule 5.025(d), after formal notice the matter proceeds "as nearly as practicable... similar to suits of a civil nature," and the Florida Rules of Civil Procedure govern (except rule 1.525), including discovery and defaults. So yes — if the estate/deed-grantee title dispute is litigated in probate at all, it gets full civil-rules treatment once declared adversary. A petition to determine homestead status (Rule 5.405) is likewise not automatically adversary but, if contested, is routinely declared adversary.

2) FORUM: PROBATE DIVISION VS. THE PENDING EJECTMENT. Both "divisions" are the same Sixth Judicial Circuit court; the division split is administrative. But the long-standing Florida doctrine is that the probate forum does not settle title disputes between the estate and a "stranger to the estate" — a person claiming property adversely to the estate rather than through it (see discussion at https://www.floridaprobatelitigationlawyer.com/removing_a_nontenant_from_esta/, noting it is "not within the purview of a probate court to settle disputes between the estate and a stranger to the estate," and that ejectment is a circuit-civil action, Fla. Stat. ch. 66, https://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0000-0099%2F0066%2F0066.html). Brandi claims UNDER A DEED, adversely to the estate — a classic stranger-to-the-estate posture. The PR himself chose the civil forum by filing ejectment. Critically, in ejectment the plaintiff must prevail on the strength of his own title/right of possession, not the weakness of the defendant's (https://www.ricelawflorida.com/recovering-possession-of-property-ejectment-action-in-florida/; https://www.coreyszalailaw.com/blog/2025/04/quiet-title-vs-ejectment-florida-differences/). Her answer and affirmative defenses asserting legal title under the delivered-but-unrecorded deed (and equitable title) squarely put the title question at issue in 26-002613-CI, and a counterclaim for declaratory relief/quiet title (ch. 86 / ch. 65) gets an affirmative adjudication of her title. CONCLUSION: the title merits should be fought in the CI case, not duplicated in probate. Filing a parallel adversary title petition in probate risks duplicative litigation, a priority/abatement fight, and the probate judge deferring to the pending civil action anyway. The probate-side filings should instead be homestead- and notice-focused (see #3, #5).

(2) Fla. Stat. 733.608(2) limits only the PR's extrajudicial custodial possession of apparently-protected homestead; Brandi's apparent-interest occupancy blocks that self-help power but is not by itself a merits defense to an ejectment in which the PR contends the deed is invalid and the property is an estate asset — homestead status and title can be adjudicated in the civil case itself, not only via Rule 5.405. **Verifier's analysis:** The statutory propositions are accurate and every citation checks out. Fla. Stat. 733.607(1) (verified at leg.state.fl.us) says the PR "has a right to, and shall take possession or control of, the decedent's property, except the protected homestead." Fla. Stat. 733.608(1) (verified) makes all property "except the protected homestead" estate assets, and 733.608(2) authorizes PR possession of apparently-protected homestead only if it "is not occupied by a person who appears to have an interest in the property," and then only for the limited purpose of preserving/insuring/protecting it pending a homestead determination. The gartenlaw.com article exists and supports the claim (citing Herrilka v. Yates, 13 So. 3d 122 (Fla. 4th DCA 2009); Harrell v. Snyder, 913 So. 2d 749 (Fla. 5th DCA 2005)). Independent authority confirms the doctrine: Clifton v. Clifton, 553 So. 2d 192, 194 n.3 (Fla. 5th DCA 1989) ("Homestead property, whether devised or not, passes outside of the probate estate. Personal representatives have no jurisdiction over nor title to homestead, and it is not an asset of the testatory estate."). So the conditional core — if the Seminole house is adjudicated "protected homestead," the PR qua PR has no title or possessory right and his ejectment fails for lack of a possessory basis — is sound law. HOWEVER, the claim is materially incomplete as applied to Brandi's facts in three respects. (1) Definitional trap: "protected homestead" under Fla. Stat. 731.201(33) (verified) exists only where the Art. X, s. 4(b) exemption "inures to the owner's surviving spouse or heirs." Brandi is neither a spouse nor an heir (731.201(20)). Her own Lady Bird deed theory is logically inconsistent with protected-homestead status: if the deed is valid, the house passed to HER outside probate by deed and is not "protected homestead" (her winning defense is superior title, not homestead); if the deed is invalid, the house is protected homestead only if it descends to the decedent's heirs — in which case title vested in the HEIRS at death, and they (not Brandi) hold the right to possession and can eject her; and if a will devised it to a non-heir or the exemption otherwise does not inure, it is NOT protected homestead, IS an estate asset, and the PR's 733.607(1) possession right is fully intact. A Rule 5.405 determination is thus a double-edged sword that can never vest possession rights in Brandi herself. (2) 733.608(2) governs the PR's extrajudicial, custodial possession power; her apparent-interest occupancy takes her outside that limited self-help power, but the subsection is not itself a merits defense to an ejectment action in which the PR contends the deed is invalid and the property is an estate asset — the circuit civil court can adjudicate title and homestead status within the ejectment, and Rule 5.405 is not the exclusive forum. (3) If plaintiff Hewett is also an heir/devisee, he could maintain (or amend to maintain) ejectment in his individual capacity even after a protected-homestead determination. **Verifier's analysis:** The claim is CONFIRMED on every material point against the actual text of Fla. Prob. R. 5.025. (1) Subdivision (a)'s automatic-adversary list contains only: proceedings to remove or surcharge a personal representative or guardian, obtain an injunction under s. 825.1035, probate a lost/destroyed/later-discovered will, determine beneficiaries, construe/reform/modify a will, cancel a devise, partition property for purposes of distribution, determine pretermitted status/share, determine elective share and contribution, and revocation of probate — a dispute over whether real property is an estate asset or passed to a Lady Bird deed grantee is NOT on that list (verified via https://www.syfert.com/florida/probate-rules/rule-5.025/ and corroborated by flcourts.gov rule compilations and the Florida Bar News article on the 2018 SC18-1459 amendment at https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-news/amendments-to-probate-rule-5-025-adversary-proceedings/; the 2018 amendment only added the 825.1035 injunction item). (2) Rule 5.025(b): other proceedings may be declared adversary by service of a separate declaration; if served by the petitioner it must be served with the petition — confirmed verbatim. (3) Rule 5.025(c): the court may determine any proceeding adversary at any time — confirmed. (4) Rule 5.025(d): petitioner must serve formal notice, and after service the proceeding is conducted as nearly as practicable like a suit of a civil nature, including entry of defaults, with the Florida Rules of Civil Procedure governing — confirmed. (ii) The claim omits that Rule 5.025(d) excepts civil rule 1.525 (motions for costs/fees), and that a RESPONDENT may also declare a proceeding adversary by serving the declaration with the initial written response — both omissions are immaterial here.
