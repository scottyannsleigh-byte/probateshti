1) ADVERSARY PROCEEDING UNDER RULE 5.025. A dispute over whether real property belongs to the estate versus a deed grantee is not one of the automatically-adversary proceedings listed in Fla. Prob. R. 5.025(a) (that list covers removal/surcharge of a PR, will construction/reformation, determination of beneficiaries, revocation of probate, elective share, etc. — see https://floridarules.net/probate/rule-5-025-adversary-proceedings/ and https://www.syfert.com/florida/probate-rules/rule-5.025/). However, under Rule 5.025(b) "other proceedings may be declared adversary" by serving a separate declaration of adversary status with the petition, and under 5.025(c) the court may declare any proceeding adversary at any time. Mechanics: she would file a verified petition (Rule 5.020 pleading standards) plus a declaration that the proceeding is adversary, and serve FORMAL NOTICE (Rule 5.040) on the PR and interested persons — formal notice is served like process. Under Rule 5.025(d), after formal notice the matter proceeds "as nearly as practicable... similar to suits of a civil nature," and the Florida Rules of Civil Procedure govern (except rule 1.525), including discovery and defaults. So yes — if the estate/deed-grantee title dispute is litigated in probate at all, it gets full civil-rules treatment once declared adversary. A petition to determine homestead status (Rule 5.405) is likewise not automatically adversary but, if contested, is routinely declared adversary.

2) FORUM: PROBATE DIVISION VS. THE PENDING EJECTMENT. Both "divisions" are the same Sixth Judicial Circuit court; the division split is administrative. But the long-standing Florida doctrine is that the probate forum does not settle title disputes between the estate and a "stranger to the estate" — a person claiming property adversely to the estate rather than through it (see discussion at https://www.floridaprobatelitigationlawyer.com/removing_a_nontenant_from_esta/, noting it is "not within the purview of a probate court to settle disputes between the estate and a stranger to the estate," and that ejectment is a circuit-civil action, Fla. Stat. ch. 66, https://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0000-0099%2F0066%2F0066.html). Brandi claims UNDER A DEED, adversely to the estate — a classic stranger-to-the-estate posture. The PR himself chose the civil forum by filing ejectment. Critically, in ejectment the plaintiff must prevail on the strength of his own title/right of possession, not the weakness of the defendant's (https://www.ricelawflorida.com/recovering-possession-of-property-ejectment-action-in-florida/; https://www.coreyszalailaw.com/blog/2025/04/quiet-title-vs-ejectment-florida-differences/). Her answer and affirmative defenses asserting legal title under the delivered-but-unrecorded deed (and equitable title) squarely put the title question at issue in 26-002613-CI, and a counterclaim for declaratory relief/quiet title (ch. 86 / ch. 65) gets an affirmative adjudication of her title. CONCLUSION: the title merits should be fought in the CI case, not duplicated in probate. Filing a parallel adversary title petition in probate risks duplicative litigation, a priority/abatement fight, and the probate judge deferring to the pending civil action anyway. The probate-side filings should instead be homestead- and notice-focused (see #3, #5).

3) CAVEAT AND THE RULE 5.405 HOMESTEAD PETITION. Caveat (Fla. Stat. 731.110, https://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0700-0799/0731/Sections/0731.110.html): a caveat by a non-creditor interested person prevents the court from admitting a will or APPOINTING a PR until formal notice is served on the caveator. It is a front-end device. Here administration is already open and Hewett already holds letters (he is suing as PR), so a caveat now is essentially moot — it does not undo an existing appointment (see https://www.zoeckleinlawpa.com/caveats-for-florida-probate/). Not useful; skip it. Petition to Determine Protected Homestead Status (Fla. Prob. R. 5.405): the rule expressly allows "an interested person" — not just the PR or heirs — to file a verified petition stating the petitioner's interest, describing the property, and (post-2024/2025 amendments) stating whether decedent was survived by a spouse, whether homestead rights were waived, and whether he died testate or intestate (https://www.courtrules.net/florida/florida-probate/rule-5-405; https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-supreme-court/117656570.html; https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-news/florida-probate-rules-committee-proposes-amendments-to-florida-probate-rule-5-405-proceedings-to-determine-protected-homestead-status-of-real-property/). Brandi's status as an "interested person" (Fla. Stat. 731.201 — one who may reasonably be expected to be affected by the outcome) is colorable: she is the record occupant, the claimed remainder grantee of the very property, and the defendant in the PR's possession suit. Expect the estate to contest her standing, but she has a good-faith basis. WHY IT MATTERS: Fla. Stat. 733.607(1) gives the PR possession of all the decedent's property "except the protected homestead" (https://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0700-0799/0733/Sections/0733.607.html), and 733.608(1) makes all property EXCEPT protected homestead an asset in the PR's hands; 733.608(2) permits the PR to take possession of apparently-protected homestead only if it "is not occupied by a person who appears to have an interest in the property," and then only to preserve/insure it pending a homestead determination (http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&Search_String=&URL=0700-0799/0733/Sections/0733.608.html; https://gartenlaw.com/articles-of-interest/can-a-personal-representative-take-possession-of-protected-homestead-property/). Because Brandi occupies the home claiming an interest, the PR's statutory possession power over even apparently-protected homestead is doubtful, and an order determining the property WAS protected homestead would knock out the PR's standing/possessory right underlying the ejectment (the ejectment plaintiff is "Hewett" as PR; the property would not be an estate asset he is entitled to possess). IMPORTANT CAVEATS: (i) If the Lady Bird deed is valid, the property is not in the estate at all — homestead analysis is a fallback that attacks the PR's authority either way (owned-by-Brandi OR protected homestead vesting in heirs at death, in neither case a PR asset). (ii) If the deed fails, "protected homestead" descends to the decedent's HEIRS at death (732.401 context), not to Brandi — a homestead determination does not give her title; its value to her is defensive (destroying the PR's possession claim and forcing any ejectment to be brought by the actual heirs on their own title). (iii) Homestead status requires the decedent to have owned and resided on the property as his homestead at death — consistent with the facts here (Art. X, §4, Fla. Const.). Also note: because decedent apparently left no surviving spouse or minor child (Brandi was an unmarried partner), the constitutional devise/alienation restrictions do not invalidate the inter vivos Lady Bird conveyance.

4) DEADLINES AND LAPSE RISKS. Her TITLE claim is not a 733.702 "claim" — the nonclaim statute reaches liabilities of the decedent (731.201 defines "claim" as liabilities and causes of action), and Florida authority treats assertions of ownership of specific property held by the PR as outside the nonclaim bar (see the Florida Law Review treatment of nonclaim exceptions, https://www.floridalawreview.com/api/v1/articles/79882-nonclaim-procedure-under-the-florida-probate-code-exceptions-to-an-apparent-statutory-bar.pdf) — confidence medium on the precise doctrinal boundary, but the deed theory is an ownership claim, not a debt. This is her most concrete lapse risk in probate: an authorized sale to a bona fide purchaser without notice could defeat her UNRECORDED deed under the recording statute, 695.01 (https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2024/Chapter695/All; https://www.epgdlaw.com/must-a-deed-be-recorded-to-be-valid-in-florida/).
